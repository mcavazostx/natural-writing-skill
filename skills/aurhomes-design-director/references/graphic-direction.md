# Graphic Design and Creative Direction

Use for thumbnails, banners, posters, ads, flyers, carousels, campaign visuals, and visual critique.

## Concept framework
Every piece needs a central idea that can be described in one sentence.

A strong concept answers:
- What tension, promise, contrast, or story makes this worth looking at?
- What visual device expresses that idea?
- What can be removed without weakening it?

## Composition
- Build around one primary focal point.
- Use scale differences aggressively enough to create hierarchy.
- Place supporting elements where they reinforce eye flow.
- Use whitespace strategically.
- Crop imagery with intent.
- Consider editorial asymmetry rather than default centering.

## Typography
- Treat the headline as a visual object.
- Use line breaks to control pacing and emphasis.
- Avoid outlining/glowing text unless essential to legibility.
- Prefer contrast through scale, weight, placement, and value.
- Keep supporting copy subordinate.

## Thumbnail mode
For YouTube thumbnails:
- Communicate the idea within roughly one second.
- Prefer one face or one property/subject plus one clear supporting device.
- Keep text very short when text is needed.
- Design for phone-size viewing.
- Use emotion and contrast without exaggerated clickbait distortion.
- Avoid cluttered arrows, circles, stickers, and effects unless they perform a specific job.
- Keep faces natural and recognizable.
- Real-estate thumbnails should make the house/location/value proposition visually obvious.

## Ad mode
- Message hierarchy: promise → proof/context → CTA.
- Keep CTA explicit.
- Build versions for placement ratios rather than blindly cropping one master.
- Do not bury the offer in decorative design.

## Carousel mode
- Slide 1 earns the swipe.
- Each slide makes one primary point.
- Maintain a repeatable grid while varying emphasis.
- Use progression: hook → explanation → proof → takeaway → CTA.
- Avoid making every slide structurally identical.

## Critique standard
Evaluate:
- Concept specificity
- Focal point
- Reading order
- Typography
- Image specificity
- Brand ownability
- Conversion clarity
- Craft details

A design fails the distinctiveness test when a logo swap could make it belong to almost any business.
